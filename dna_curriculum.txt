# DNA CURRICULUM - Biology Educational Module
# Sources: NCBI, OpenStax Biology, Khan Academy
# Topic: Cell Biology & Molecular Genetics

## SECTION 1: DNA STRUCTURE AND MOLECULAR COMPOSITION

**Nucleotide Structure (OpenStax Biology)**
DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a polymer composed of monomers called nucleotides. Each nucleotide consists of three components: a five-carbon sugar called deoxyribose, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base. The deoxyribose sugar is bonded to both the phosphate group and the nitrogenous base. The deoxyribose sugar is located in the center of the nucleotide and is bonded to the phosphate group on one side (the 5' carbon) and to the nitrogenous base on the other side (the 1' carbon).

**The Double Helix Structure (NCBI)**
DNA exists as a double helix structure, which was discovered by Watson, Crick, Franklin, and Wilkins in 1953. The double helix model describes DNA as two antiparallel strands wound around each other in a spiral formation. Each strand contains a sugar-phosphate backbone on the outside and nitrogenous bases on the inside. The two strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs: adenine (A) pairs with thymine (T) via two hydrogen bonds, and guanine (G) pairs with cytosine (C) via three hydrogen bonds.

**Nitrogenous Bases (Khan Academy)**
There are four types of nitrogenous bases in DNA: two purines (adenine and guanine) and two pyrimidines (cytosine and thymine). Purines are larger, double-ring structures containing nine atoms in their ring system. Pyrimidines are smaller, single-ring structures containing six atoms. The complementary base pairing rules are strict: purines always pair with pyrimidines, which maintains uniform diameter of the DNA double helix.

**Base Pairing and Chargaff's Rules**
Erwin Chargaff discovered that the amount of adenine equals the amount of thymine, and the amount of guanine equals the amount of cytosine in DNA. This 1:1 ratio is called Chargaff's rules and supported the Watson-Crick model of base pairing. The hydrogen bonds between base pairs are easily broken, allowing for strand separation during replication and transcription.

## SECTION 2: DNA REPLICATION

**Semi-Conservative Replication (OpenStax Biology)**
DNA replication is described as semi-conservative because each new DNA molecule consists of one original strand and one newly synthesized strand. This mechanism was demonstrated by Meselson and Stahl using heavy isotope labeling. The parental strands separate and each serves as a template for the synthesis of a new complementary strand. During replication, the double helix unwinds, and the hydrogen bonds between base pairs are broken, allowing the two strands to separate.

**DNA Polymerase and Enzyme Catalysis (NCBI)**
DNA polymerase is the primary enzyme responsible for synthesizing new DNA strands. It catalyzes the formation of phosphodiester bonds between nucleotides, adding them to the 3' end of the growing DNA strand. DNA polymerase requires a primer (a short RNA sequence synthesized by primase) to initiate synthesis and cannot start synthesis de novo. The enzyme moves along the template strand in the 3' to 5' direction, reading the bases and adding complementary nucleotides.

**Leading and Lagging Strands (Khan Academy)**
Because DNA polymerase can only synthesize in the 5' to 3' direction and the two strands are antiparallel, one strand (leading strand) is synthesized continuously as the replication fork opens. The other strand (lagging strand) is synthesized discontinuously in short segments called Okazaki fragments, which are typically 1000-2000 nucleotides in prokaryotes and 100-200 nucleotides in eukaryotes. These fragments are later joined by DNA ligase.

**DNA Replication Process Timeline**
The replication of the entire human genome (approximately 3 billion base pairs) takes approximately 8 hours during the S phase of the cell cycle. The replication rate is approximately 50 nucleotides per second in prokaryotes and 50 base pairs per second in eukaryotes. Multiple origins of replication along each chromosome allow simultaneous replication of different regions, dramatically reducing the overall replication time.

## SECTION 3: GENE EXPRESSION - TRANSCRIPTION AND TRANSLATION

**Transcription Overview (OpenStax Biology)**
Transcription is the process by which information encoded in DNA is transcribed into a complementary RNA molecule. RNA polymerase II (in eukaryotes) or RNA polymerase (in prokaryotes) binds to the promoter region of a gene and unwinds the double helix. As the enzyme moves along the template strand in the 3' to 5' direction, it adds complementary ribonucleotides to form messenger RNA (mRNA).

**mRNA Processing in Eukaryotes (NCBI)**
In eukaryotes, the primary transcript (pre-mRNA) undergoes three major modifications: 5' capping, 3' polyadenylation, and splicing. The 5' cap is a 7-methylguanosine added to the 5' end, which protects the mRNA and aids in ribosome binding. The 3' poly-A tail, consisting of approximately 200 adenine nucleotides, stabilizes the mRNA and aids in translation. Splicing removes non-coding introns and joins coding exons to create mature mRNA.

**Translation Process (Khan Academy)**
Translation is the process by which mRNA is decoded to synthesize proteins. The ribosome binds to the mRNA at the start codon (AUG) and moves along the sequence reading codons in the 5' to 3' direction. Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules bring amino acids to the ribosome, where they are added to the growing protein chain. Each tRNA has an anticodon that is complementary to a specific mRNA codon, ensuring correct amino acid incorporation.

## SECTION 4: MUTATIONS AND GENETIC VARIATION

**Types of Mutations (OpenStax Biology)**
Point mutations are changes in a single nucleotide. Substitution mutations replace one base with another (silent, missense, or nonsense). Insertion mutations add one or more nucleotides, while deletion mutations remove nucleotides. These mutations can shift the reading frame (frameshift mutations), dramatically altering the protein produced. Larger mutations include inversions (reversal of DNA segment) and translocations (movement of DNA segment to another location).

**Mutation Causes and Prevention (NCBI)**
Mutations can result from DNA replication errors, environmental mutagens (UV radiation, chemicals), or spontaneous damage. Cells have proofreading mechanisms: DNA polymerase has 3' to 5' exonuclease activity for immediate error correction, and mismatch repair systems detect and fix errors after replication. Despite these mechanisms, approximately one mutation per 10^9 nucleotides persists in humans per generation.

**Natural Selection and Evolution (Khan Academy)**
Most mutations are neutral, some are beneficial, and some are deleterious. Natural selection acts on genetic variation, with beneficial mutations increasing in frequency over generations. This process drives evolutionary change and adaptation. The genetic diversity resulting from mutation and recombination provides raw material for evolution.

## SECTION 5: GENE REGULATION AND EPIGENETICS

**Transcriptional Control (OpenStax Biology)**
Gene expression is controlled at multiple levels, with transcriptional regulation being the most efficient. Transcription factors are proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences (promoters and enhancers) to regulate RNA polymerase activity. These regulatory proteins can activate (positive regulation) or inhibit (negative regulation) transcription. Chromatin structure also affects transcriptional accessibility: tightly packed heterochromatin is less accessible, while loosely packed euchromatin is more accessible.

**DNA Methylation (NCBI)**
DNA methylation occurs when methyl groups (CH3) are added to cytosine bases, typically in CpG dinucleotides. Methylated DNA is generally associated with gene silencing, while unmethylated DNA is accessible for transcription. This epigenetic mark can be maintained through cell divisions and is reversible, allowing for dynamic gene regulation without changing the DNA sequence.

**Histone Modifications and Chromatin Remodeling (Khan Academy)**
DNA wraps around histone proteins to form nucleosomes, which can be further packaged into higher-order chromatin structures. Histone tails are modified through acetylation, methylation, and phosphorylation. Histone acetylation generally loosens chromatin structure and activates transcription, while histone methylation can activate or repress transcription depending on the specific histone residue modified.
